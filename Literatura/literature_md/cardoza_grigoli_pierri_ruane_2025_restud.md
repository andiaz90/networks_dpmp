## What they do

Matched employer–employee data joined to the **universe of firm-to-firm
transactions** for the Dominican Republic. Five documented facts:

1. Workers move between buyers and suppliers **almost twice as much** as standard
   labour-market characteristics predict.
2. Movers between buyers and suppliers get **larger earnings increases** than
   other movers.
3. **Incumbent** workers' earnings rise when their firm hires from its buyers or
   suppliers.
4. Firm-to-firm trade **increases following** supply-chain hires.
5. Hiring from buyers or suppliers is associated with **stronger firm growth**.

## Why it matters for NK-IOSOE

This is the empirical case that labour reallocation is **not orthogonal to the
production network** — workers flow along the same edges as goods. Three uses:

1. **Motivation for the labour adjustment cost.** Our Ψ_l is currently justified
   only by citing FGI. This paper gives an independent, micro-data reason why
   sectoral labour reallocation is neither free nor random: it is structured by
   the IO network. A single sentence in the introduction.
2. **It cuts against a uniform reallocation cost.** If workers move most easily
   between network neighbours, the right friction is a *matrix* c_ij, not a
   scalar c. We assume a scalar (and ψ_l = 0 for the sectoral component). Worth
   acknowledging as a limitation rather than waiting for a referee.
3. **Emerging-economy evidence.** The Dominican Republic is a closer comparator
   for Chile than US or euro-area micro data, which matters if we claim external
   relevance for a Chilean calibration.

## Caveats

- Purely empirical — no structural model, no counterfactual. Do not cite it as
  support for any *quantitative* magnitude in our model.
- Body unread. Verify the exact identification (event studies around hires?
  firm-level panel?) before making any causal claim on its behalf.
- Check the published REStud version against the STEG working paper: the fact
  list may have changed between May 2023 and the November 2025 issue.
