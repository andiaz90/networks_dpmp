\subsection{Oil Price Shock}\label{sec:oil_shock}

We now use the model to study the transmission of a world oil price shock through the Chilean economy.
This exercise is motivated by Chile's status as a net oil importer: crude oil and refined fuels constitute a significant fraction of intermediate imports across most sectors, with particularly high exposure in Transport and Communications (37\%), Mining (22\%), Manufacturing (19\%), and Agriculture (16\%).
Understanding how oil price shocks propagate is especially relevant for monetary policy, since the input-output structure of production implies that the first-round cost increase in oil-intensive sectors spills over to downstream sectors through intermediate input trade---a channel that is absent in representative-agent models without production networks.

\subsubsection{Experiment Design}

We subject the economy to a one-time 10\% increase in the world oil price $P^{O*}_t$, governed by the AR(1) process
\begin{equation}
    \log\left(\frac{P^{O*}_t}{P^{O*}}\right) = \rho_{O^*}\,\log\left(\frac{P^{O*}_{t-1}}{P^{O*}}\right) + \sigma_{O^*}\,\varepsilon^{O^*}_t,
    \label{eq:oil_process}
\end{equation}
with persistence $\rho_{O^*} = 0.9$ and innovation standard deviation $\sigma_{O^*} = 0.02$.
All other shocks are set to zero.
The domestic oil price is determined by full exchange rate pass-through, $P^O_t = \mathcal{E}_t P^{O*}_t$, so that movements in the real exchange rate partially offset or amplify the foreign-currency oil price change.

In each sector $i$, composite intermediate imports $V_{it}$ are a CES aggregate of oil ($V^{\text{Oil}}_{it}$) and non-oil ($V^{\text{Non}}_{it}$) imports with sector-specific oil shares $\alpha^{\text{Oil}}_i$ drawn from Chile's 2021 Input-Output tables:\footnote{Specifically, we use the use matrix (Cuadro 21) for products 28 and 77--81 (crude oil, diesel, gasoline, kerosene, fuel oils, LPG) at basic prices, aggregated to the 12-sector classification.}
\begin{equation}
    P^{V}_{it}{}^{1-\epsilon_{V}^{\text{oil}}} = \alpha^{\text{Oil}}_i\, (P^O_t)^{1-\epsilon_{V}^{\text{oil}}} + (1-\alpha^{\text{Oil}}_i)\, (P^v_t)^{1-\epsilon_{V}^{\text{oil}}},
    \label{eq:PIV_oil}
\end{equation}
with elasticity of substitution between oil and non-oil imports $\epsilon_V^{\text{oil}} = 0.5$, reflecting limited short-run substitutability.

\subsubsection{Transmission Channels}

The oil price shock transmits to the economy through two distinct channels.

\paragraph{Direct cost channel.}
A higher oil price raises the composite import price index $P^{V}_{it}$ in every sector.
Because imported inputs enter the production function with share $\alpha_{Vi}$, the first-round impact on marginal cost is approximately
\begin{equation}
    \Delta \log MC^i_t \bigg|_{\text{direct}} \approx \alpha_{Vi}\,\alpha^{\text{Oil}}_i\,\Delta \log P^O_t.
    \label{eq:direct_mc}
\end{equation}
This effect is heterogeneous across sectors: Transport and Communications faces a direct cost increase roughly nine times larger than Financial Services, reflecting the cross-sectoral dispersion in oil intensity documented in Figure~\ref{fig:oil_intensity}.

\paragraph{Network amplification channel.}
The direct cost increase in oil-intensive sectors raises their output prices $P^H_{it}$, which in turn enters the intermediate input cost of every sector purchasing from them through the IO matrix $\Gamma_{i,j}$.
This indirect effect propagates recursively through the production network.
Using a first-order approximation, the total effect on marginal costs---including all rounds of network propagation---is given by the Leontief inverse:
\begin{equation}
    \Delta \log \mathbf{MC}\bigg|_{\text{total}} = \left(I - \text{diag}(\boldsymbol{\alpha}_m)\,\Gamma\right)^{-1}\,\Delta \log \mathbf{MC}\bigg|_{\text{direct}},
    \label{eq:leontief_mc}
\end{equation}
where $\boldsymbol{\alpha}_m$ is the vector of material input shares and $\Gamma$ is the IO coefficient matrix.
The network amplification effect is then the difference between total and direct effects.

\subsubsection{Aggregate Responses}

Figure~\ref{fig:oil_agg_irf} shows the impulse responses of aggregate variables to the 10\% oil price shock.
\textcolor{red}{[Values to be filled after running oil\_shock\_analysis.jl.]}
GDP contracts on impact as the cost shock raises production costs economy-wide.
CPI inflation rises, reflecting the pass-through of higher import prices into domestic prices through both the direct oil channel and the network multiplier.
The real exchange rate depreciates, amplifying the domestic-currency oil price increase, while the trade balance deteriorates as the oil import bill rises.
The central bank responds by tightening the policy rate, which partially offsets the inflationary pressure but deepens the output contraction.
The persistence of the shock ($\rho_{O^*} = 0.9$) implies that effects are long-lived, with GDP returning to steady state only gradually.

\subsubsection{Sectoral Responses}

The heterogeneous oil exposure across sectors generates a rich pattern of cross-sectoral responses.
Figure~\ref{fig:oil_sec_y} displays the output IRFs for all 12 sectors.
The sectors most affected are those with the highest oil intensity of imported inputs---Transport and Communications, Mining, and Manufacturing---which face the largest direct cost increases.
However, the ranking of sectoral responses does not perfectly mirror the ranking of oil shares: sectors with low direct oil exposure but high reliance on intermediate inputs from oil-intensive sectors (such as Trade and Business Services) experience significant output contractions through the network channel.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.95\textwidth]{figures/oil_shock/irf_sectoral_Y_oil_shock.pdf}
    \caption{Sectoral output responses to a 10\% oil price shock. Blue panels are goods sectors; red panels are services sectors.}
    \label{fig:oil_sec_y}
\end{figure}

Figure~\ref{fig:oil_sec_ph} shows the sectoral price responses.
Sectors with stickier prices (higher $\kappa_i$) adjust prices more slowly, absorbing a larger fraction of the cost shock through markups in the short run.
This heterogeneity in price adjustment speeds, combined with the IO structure, implies that the inflationary impulse propagates unevenly across sectors---a feature that is central to the aggregate inflation dynamics in the model and that a one-sector model would miss entirely.

\subsubsection{Decomposition: Direct Cost vs.\ Network Amplification}

Table~\ref{tab:oil_decomp} decomposes the marginal cost increase in each sector into the direct oil cost channel and the network amplification channel, as defined in equations~\eqref{eq:direct_mc}--\eqref{eq:leontief_mc}.
Two key findings emerge.

First, the network amplification is quantitatively large.
\textcolor{red}{[Aggregate amplification ratio to be filled after running the script.]}
On an output-weighted basis, the total effect on marginal costs is substantially larger than the direct effect alone, implying that ignoring IO linkages would significantly understate the inflationary impact of oil shocks.

Second, the network channel partially reshuffles the cross-sectoral ranking of vulnerability.
While Transport and Communications has the highest direct oil exposure, sectors like Agriculture and Manufacturing receive a large additional cost push through the IO network because they purchase heavily from other oil-intensive sectors.
The amplification ratio is highest for sectors with low direct oil exposure but high upstream dependence on oil-intensive suppliers---these are the sectors for which the network channel matters most relative to the direct channel.

Figure~\ref{fig:oil_decomp_mc} provides a visual decomposition: the orange bars represent the direct oil cost effect and the blue bars represent the additional network amplification, stacked to show the total marginal cost increase per sector.

\subsubsection{The Role of Sectoral Heterogeneity}

The interaction between heterogeneous oil exposure, heterogeneous price stickiness, and the IO network creates transmission patterns that would be absent in either a representative-sector model or a multi-sector model without IO linkages.

Figure~\ref{fig:oil_intensity} documents the oil import shares across sectors.
The dispersion is substantial: Transport and Communications allocates 37\% of its imported inputs to oil, while Financial Services allocates less than 1\%.
This variation, combined with the complementarity between oil and non-oil imports ($\epsilon_V^{\text{oil}} = 0.5 < 1$), implies that oil-intensive sectors cannot easily substitute away from oil, making the cost pass-through nearly proportional to the oil share.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.85\textwidth]{figures/oil_shock/oil_intensity_exposure.pdf}
    \caption{Oil share of intermediate imports by sector ($\alpha^{\text{Oil}}_i$), computed from Chile's 2021 IO tables.}
    \label{fig:oil_intensity}
\end{figure}

The network amplification effect depends crucially on the density of the IO matrix.
Sectors that are central in the production network---those that supply intermediate inputs to many other sectors---act as amplifiers of the oil shock even if their own oil exposure is moderate.
Conversely, sectors at the periphery of the IO network experience effects closer to the direct channel alone.
This finding echoes the theoretical results in \citet{baqaee2019macroeconomic} on the role of network centrality in shock propagation, but here we quantify the channel in a calibrated model with realistic Chilean IO linkages and price rigidities.
